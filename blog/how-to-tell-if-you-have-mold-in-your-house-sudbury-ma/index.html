How to Tell If You Have Mold in Your House in Sudbury, MA

A step-by-step way to check for mold, from your nose to a moisture meter to a paper trail.

Call (978) 252-8637

By the Sudbury Water Damage Pros team. Updated October 29, 2026.

To tell if you have mold in your house in Sudbury, MA, look for three things together: a musty smell, visible growth or staining, and a source of moisture. Mold detection is the process of confirming growth and locating the water behind it.

No single clue proves it. This guide gives you a sequence of checks you can run in an afternoon, then explains when outside testing makes sense.

Step one: trust your nose, but verify

A persistent earthy or damp-sock smell is the most common first sign. It often shows up in a closed basement, a bedroom closet on an exterior wall, or a bathroom that never fully dries.

Smell alone is not proof. Old carpet, a wet dog bed and a dirty drain can smell similar. Treat the odor as a prompt to look and measure. Note which room is strongest and whether it changes after rain or a long shower.

Try the closet test. Close a bedroom closet door overnight, then open it first thing and breathe in. Stale air carries odors that a ventilated room hides, and a sharp musty note on an outside wall is worth chasing with a meter.

Step two: look for what mold leaves behind

Visible growth appears as fuzzy, speckled or smeared patches in white, green, brown, gray or black. Water stains, bubbling paint, warped baseboards, curling wallpaper and ceiling rings are also signs of past or present moisture.

Use a flashlight held at a low angle to a wall. Raking light shows texture changes that normal room light hides. Photograph anything suspicious with a coin or tape measure in the frame so the size is clear.

Step three: use a moisture meter

A moisture meter is the most useful single tool. Pin-type meters read wood and drywall by probing, while pinless meters scan the surface. Take readings on a wall you know is dry first, then compare. Numbers that stay well above your dry reference point to trapped water.

A thermal imaging camera adds another angle. Wet materials often look cooler on screen than dry ones next to them. It does not detect mold. It shows temperature patterns that suggest where to measure. Both tools together can trace a leak without cutting into the wall.

Readings need a baseline

Different materials read differently. Compare a suspect area with a dry spot of the same material, not with a number from the internet.

Hidden places worth checking

Mold often grows where nobody looks. Pull furniture off exterior walls and check the back. Open every vanity and kitchen sink cabinet. Look at the underside of the roof sheathing in the attic, especially around bath fan ducts and chimney chases.

In a crawl space, check the soil cover, the insulation and the sill plate. Behind a refrigerator or washing machine, look for a damp floor or stained drywall. Closets packed tight against cold walls also trap moisture.

A garage-attached bonus room or a finished basement bathroom deserves special attention. Both often have little airflow, plumbing in the walls and carpet over concrete that can wick moisture upward.

  • Under sinks and around supply lines
  • Attic sheathing near vents and fan ducts

When third-party testing makes sense

Testing is a tool, not a requirement. It can help when you smell mold but cannot find it, when you are buying or selling, or when there is a dispute. An independent tester takes air or surface samples and sends them to a lab.

Independent testers have no stake in the cleanup, which many people prefer. Ask any testing firm what it will sample and how it reports. Visible growth with an obvious leak often needs no test to justify the repair. Mold remediation contractors generally work from the moisture findings first.

Clearance testing after cleanup

Clearance testing is a check after remediation to confirm that conditions look normal. It is generally done by a third party, not the crew that did the work. The published ANSI/IICRC S520 standard describes verification steps that include visual inspection and, in some cases, sampling.

If you want clearance testing, say so before work starts so the scope and timing can include it. This page describes the process in general only, and does not claim that any single company performs it.

Document everything as you go

Write down dates, rooms, smells and readings. Take dated photos before anything is cleaned or removed. Save receipts for dehumidifiers, repairs and testing, and keep any lab reports in one folder.

A clear record helps contractors scope the work and helps when you talk to your insurer. If a leak is active, shut off the water and have water mitigation start drying before growth spreads further.

Add a simple log of humidity readings taken at the same time each day for a week. A hygrometer reading that climbs after showers, laundry or rain shows when moisture is entering, which helps a technician narrow the cause.

Local note

What Sudbury homeowners should check after a wet season

Sudbury has the Sudbury River on its southeast edge and Hop Brook running through town, so spring snowmelt and fall storms can raise groundwater around lower foundations. After a wet stretch, walk your basement and crawl space and check readings at the lowest points. Check the FEMA flood map for your address if you are unsure of your exposure.

Because most homes use onsite septic, as the Town of Sudbury's wastewater planning page describes, a musty smell with slow drains or soggy yard soil may point to the septic system. Nearby towns such as Maynard and Concord see the same seasonal pattern.

Frequently asked questions

What does mold smell like?

Most people describe an earthy, damp or stale odor, like a wet basement or old towels. Smell varies with the material and the amount of growth.

An odor without visible growth often means mold is hidden behind a wall, under flooring or in a cabinet.

Can I use a home mold test kit?

Kits can show that spores exist, but spores are present in nearly every home and outdoors. A kit usually cannot tell you the cause, the size of the problem or what to do next.

A moisture meter and a careful inspection tell you more about where to act.

How do I find mold I cannot see?

Look for moisture instead. Use a moisture meter, a thermal imaging camera and a flashlight, and check under sinks, behind furniture, in the attic and in crawl spaces.

A technician may use an inspection camera through a small hole to look inside a wall.

Do I need independent testing before hiring a remediation company?

Not necessarily. If growth is visible and the water source is known, a scope of work can be written without it. Independent testing is more common when the cause is unclear, or for clearance afterward.

Ask any company whether state or local rules apply to your project.

How do I document mold for an insurance claim?

Take dated photos and video, note readings and the cause if known, and keep receipts. Do not throw away damaged items until you have photographed them.

Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered, and gradual leaks often are not, so check your policy and ask your insurer.

Could a flooded Sudbury River basement hide mold for weeks?

Yes. After river or groundwater flooding, water can sit in wall cavities and under flooring while the surface looks dry. Mold may begin to grow in damp, covered areas within a few days.

Flood is usually excluded from a standard homeowners policy, so ask your insurer or agent about separate flood coverage.
