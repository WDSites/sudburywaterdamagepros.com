How Long Does Water Damage Take to Dry in a Sudbury, MA Home?

Drying ends when the readings say so, not when the calendar does.

Call (978) 252-8637

By the Sudbury Water Damage Pros team. Updated October 29, 2026.

Water damage drying time is the span between the first extraction of water and the moment moisture readings in every affected material return to a normal dry range. In a Sudbury, MA home, a small clean leak and a flooded finished basement can differ by many days.

People often ask for one number. The honest answer is a general range plus the reasons it moves. This guide walks through the drying phases, the equipment, how different materials behave and what the S500 standard expects.

Drying happens in four phases

A professional drying job follows a sequence. Skipping a step usually costs more time later.

  • Extraction: pumps and extraction units remove standing and pooled water first.
  • Setup: air movers, LGR dehumidifiers and sometimes HEPA air scrubbers are placed using a moisture map.
  • Monitoring: technicians record readings daily and adjust equipment as walls and floors release water.
  • Verification: final readings are compared against a dry standard before equipment leaves.

What each piece of equipment does

Air movers push dry air across wet surfaces and help water leave the material as vapor. They work on the surface, so they are paired with something that pulls the vapor out of the room.

That partner is the LGR dehumidifier, short for low grain refrigerant. It removes moisture from the air even when the air is already fairly dry, which keeps the drying going. Moisture meters read what is inside the wood, drywall or concrete, and a thermal imaging camera shows cool, wet patches behind finished surfaces.

Why 3 to 5 days is a general range and not a promise

For a modest clean-water loss that is caught fast, structural drying often runs about three to five days. Treat that as a general range only. It is not a promise for your house.

The range stretches when water reached several rooms, soaked thick materials, sat for a day or more, or came in during humid weather. It shrinks when the area is small and extraction is quick. A reliable company will give you a plan based on your readings and update it daily.

Moisture readings and drying goals

The goal is not zero moisture. It is a dry standard, meaning the reading in the wet material matches the reading in a similar material that was never wet. Technicians pick a reference spot in an unaffected room and compare.

Readings are logged each visit, so you can see the numbers fall. If a wall stops dropping, the crew changes something, such as opening a cavity, adding an air mover or relocating a dehumidifier. Ask to see the log. It is also useful for your insurer.

Different materials have different dry numbers, so a single reading never settles the question. A wood stud, a drywall sheet and a concrete slab each get their own target, which is one reason jobs finish room by room instead of all at once.

What the S500 standard asks for

ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published industry standard for water damage restoration, and reputable companies follow it. It sets out water categories, drying principles and the idea of documenting conditions at the start, during and at the finish.

In practice that means moisture mapping, psychrometric readings of temperature and humidity, and a drying plan that is reviewed as conditions change. For the setup phase see water mitigation, which describes the first 24 to 72 hours.

What stretches the timeline

A few repeat offenders explain most long jobs. Knowing them helps you ask smart questions early.

  • Category 2 or 3 water, which requires removal of porous materials before drying.
  • Hidden pockets behind cabinets, under tubs or inside insulated walls.
  • Delay between the loss and extraction, which lets water soak deeper.
  • Closed doors or cooler rooms that keep equipment from working evenly.
  • Concrete floors with carpet or tile that trap moisture beneath.

A typical job, day by day

Every house is different, so read this as a sketch and not a schedule. On day one, crews extract water, map moisture and set equipment. On day two, they take readings, adjust air movers and open any cavity that is not responding.

By days three and four, drywall and framing readings often fall sharply, while thicker materials trail behind. Day five is commonly the verification visit when a loss is small and caught fast. Larger or Category 2 and 3 losses can run longer, and the crew should tell you why when the plan changes.

What to do before the crew arrives

You can protect the drying job before equipment shows up. These steps are safe for most households and are general guidance.

Stop the source, then keep people and pets out of any water that touched sewage or came from a river. Stay out of rooms where water reached outlets or a panel until the power question is settled.

  • Shut off the fixture or main water valve.
  • Open interior doors and closets so air can move later.
  • Lift furniture off wet carpet and wipe legs dry, then slip foil under them.
  • Do not turn up the heat to dry a wet wall faster, because that can drive moisture deeper.
  • Photograph the wet areas and take moisture notes if you own a meter.

What you can do while equipment runs

Leave the equipment alone, because unplugging a dehumidifier to use an outlet can add a day. Keep doors and windows closed unless the crew says otherwise, since humid summer air works against drying.

Expect noise and some added heat, and expect your electric bill to rise a little. When the readings reach goal, rebuilding can begin through water damage repair. If you smell must after drying, mention it right away so mold can be checked.

Local note

Sudbury seasons and river-adjacent drying

New England swings from freezing winters to humid summers, and drying behaves differently in each. In January, heating makes the air dry but a burst pipe can soak the same wall cavity for hours. In July, outdoor humidity is high, so keep windows shut and let dehumidifiers do the work.

After a river event the ground stays saturated for longer. The National Weather Service reported record crests at several Massachusetts river gauges in March 2010, and soil near rivers can hold water long after the surface looks clear. If your home is near the Sudbury River, ask the crew to check crawlspaces and the slab edge, not just the walls.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just run fans for a few days instead of hiring a crew?

Fans dry surfaces and push humid air around, but they cannot pull moisture out of wall cavities or concrete. Without dehumidification the air stays damp.

Moisture meters tell you whether the structure is dry. Fans alone cannot.

How do I know when my house is actually dry?

The crew should show readings that match a dry reference material and document them. A dry feel or smell is not proof.

Ask for the final moisture log before equipment is removed.

Why does my hardwood floor keep cupping after the water is gone?

Boards absorb moisture from below and swell at the edges. They may keep cupping until the subfloor and wood balance out, which can take weeks.

Sanding or replacing too early can lock in problems, so wait for dry readings.

Does drying prevent mold?

Mold needs moisture, so fast, complete drying reduces the conditions it needs. It cannot be promised away, and lingering damp areas can still grow mold.

If mold appears, testing and removal follow the S520 standard under mold remediation.

Will drying take longer in summer or winter?

Summer humidity slows drying and calls for tighter control of the room. Winter heat helps, though cold exterior walls and burst-pipe spread can create their own problems.

A crew with psychrometric readings can adjust either way.

My Sudbury house is near the river. Will drying take longer?

Possibly, if the ground stays saturated or groundwater enters the basement again. Wet soil keeps moisture flowing toward the foundation.

Ask the crew about sump function, slab readings and a later recheck after the next heavy rain.
